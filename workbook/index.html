1

What your savings actually pay

A balance is not an income. The question is how much it can pay you every month without running dry. That depends almost entirely on the withdrawal rate.

The withdrawal rate is the share of your savings you take out in the first year. Many planners use 3% to 4% as a cautious range for a retirement that may last thirty years. These are before tax.

Worth checking: what share of the savings are in pre-tax accounts. Every pre-tax dollar you withdraw is taxed as income.
2

When you claim Social Security

Claiming at 62 cuts your check for life. Waiting to 70 raises it for life. The difference between the two is often larger than people expect, and it's permanent.

Find your benefit at full retirement age on your statement at ssa.gov/myaccount. The average retired worker collects about $2,071 a month in 2026.

What Medicare takes first

From 65, the Part B premium comes straight out of your Social Security before it's deposited. You never see that money, and it has been growing faster than your raises.

Standard premium for 2026.
Medigap, drug plan, Advantage premium.
Worth checking: every December your new benefit, the Medicare deduction and the deposit are on your notice. Write all three down, every year.

5

One bad health year

Every Medicare plan has a worst case. It's written in the plan documents, and it lands on your savings in a single year. Put a number on it before it happens.

For 2027, a Medicare Advantage plan can cap in-network costs at up to $9,850, and drug costs are capped at $2,400. That's $12,250 in one year. Find your own plan's limit in its Summary of Benefits.

Worth checking: open enrollment runs October 15 to December 7. Compare the maximum out-of-pocket, not just the premium.
